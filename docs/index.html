A note from the author

Why This Guide Exists

The Ledger hacks were what pushed me to build this guide. I’ve been in Bitcoin since 2021. My first hardware wallet was a Ledger Nano X, and I held most of my stack in self-custody on it. When the hacks hit the news, I got tired of wondering if I would be next and checking every day that I still had my Bitcoin.

That fear pushed me to build my own secure self-custody setup and write down what I learned. I have always wanted to be a web developer, and I love to teach. Sharing what I figured out for myself was the natural next step.

I want this guide to help you understand your choices, check the tools you use, protect your backups, and practice recovery. It is absolutely free to use. The goal is a setup you understand and can take responsibility for.

THE PLEB’S WORKBENCHA guide to self-custody

A guide to Bitcoin self-custody

Hardware signers are optional.

The complete interactive guide works offline.

Plebum Dominium PLAY-boom doh-MIN-ee-oom A Latin-inspired name. “Pleb” refers to an everyday Bitcoiner and draws on Latin plebs, the common people. Dominium means ownership or dominion. The name expresses “Ownership by the common people.” Here, that means holding your own Bitcoin and controlling the keys. teaches self-custody without a hardware wallet. Both paths use offline software. A hardware wallet is an optional signing tool only after the metal backups are complete and checked.

Install any required external software before disconnecting. External links, submitting feedback and broadcasting transactions need a connection. Use public test data while the project remains under review.

You do the work

Self-custody takes proof of work.

Reading is the beginning. Like Bitcoin, this guide puts work and verification at the center. Here, your proof is hands-on practice: you generate the entropy, verify the tools, make the metal backups and practice recovery. Confidence comes from doing the work and checking the results.

Roll your own entropy

You roll the dice and use the offline converter to turn the results into seed words. Learn what the rolls contribute and check the output.

Verify the tools

You check downloads, software sources and wallet details. Follow the checks yourself and compare the results with what the guide says to expect.

Make the metal backups

You put the recovery information on metal, check it and plan separate storage. A hardware wallet is optional for signing only after the metal backups are complete and checked.

Practice getting it back

You restore, receive and spend with test funds. Rehearse recovery and handover so the plan still works when the original setup is gone.

Two approaches

Choose the tradeoffs you can manage

More components can distribute risk and also create more ways to make a mistake. Neither setup is automatically the safest for everyone.

One key + a passphrase

A simpler signing arrangement with an additional recovery secret. Both the seed backup and exact passphrase must remain available.

Watch for: a forgotten passphrase, compromised signer, or backups that are stored together.

Three keys · 2-of-3

Two signing keys authorize a spend. Proper separation can tolerate one unavailable key, but recovery also depends on the complete wallet configuration.

Watch for: shared-device compromise, missing public-key records, and recovery complexity.

Dice can supply seed entropy when used correctly. They do not make a compromised computer or signing implementation safe.
